Power Grid and Input Lag

1. Power Grids: Then and Now

In the past, household appliances used linear (transformer-based) power supplies. They consumed more current but didn't "pollute" the outlet with high-frequency noise.

Today, almost everything — chargers, LED bulbs, PC power supplies — uses Switching Power Supplies (SMPS). They dump electrical "noise" back into the home network. If your home has poor wiring and no grounding, this "garbage" flows directly into your PC.

Your PC's power supply (PSU) has filters, but this noise needs to be discharged somewhere. Normally, it goes to the ground. Without it, the noise "wanders" through the case and leaks into the USB port contacts.

"As a result, interference hits your mouse controllers directly. This creates signal micro-distortions that we perceive as an unstable, 'floaty' or 'muddy' response."

2. Why were old mice different?

Older devices featured massive metal plates for shielding and ferrite beads on the cables. In the pursuit of minimal weight, modern manufacturers strip away everything "extra."

"We gain maneuverability, but the device becomes defenseless against interference from a poor power grid without GROUNDING."

3. The Paradox of Progress

Older mice operated at 125Hz — interference is less noticeable there. Modern 1000-8000Hz rates are far more sensitive to micro-fluctuations in the network. Add to that Wi-Fi and 5G noise, which creates latency that didn't exist 15 years ago.

4. Lab Tests vs. Reality

Almost all reviews are conducted in ideal conditions using $1000+ power conditioners. In reality, with old wiring, a top-tier $150 sensor can turn into a "floaty" tool. Poor electrical infrastructure fundamentally changes how a device behaves.

5. Data Distortion

When there is too much noise in the network, the mouse controller spends vital microseconds on error correction. The gamer feels this as a loss of responsiveness.

A key sign: If you periodically feel "shocks" or static from your PC case — the PSU has nowhere to discharge electromagnetic interference.

6. Why lack of grounding kills your "hitreg"?

If your PC "stings" when you touch it, that’s a potential of about 110V. For a microchip, this is like someone constantly shaking a set of high-precision scales while you're trying to weigh a milligram of powder.

How it works technically:

• Micro-errors: Sensor data arrives with distortions.
• Time Loss: The processor spends microseconds correcting those errors.
• Input Lag: A feeling of "dragging through mud" or "aim drift" appears.

How dangerous is this?
For Health: If a real short circuit occurs in the PSU, the PC case becomes a live wire.
For Hardware: This potential slowly "cooks" the USB controllers and motherboard ports.
